## Section — Dewatering

**Heading:** `Construction Dewatering`

- **Sump and pump dewatering** — the workhorse method for trench and excavation dewatering
- **Wellpoint dewatering** — where groundwater has to be drawn down ahead of excavation rather than pumped from the hole
- **Deep well and bypass pumping** — larger drawdown and higher volume applications
- **Sewer and watercourse bypass pumping** — keeping a live system flowing while the work proceeds
- **Excavation and trench dewatering** — sustained through deep and high-water-table installations
- **Pond and impoundment dewatering** — settling ponds, containment and process water impoundments
- **Mine dewatering support** — pit, sump and pond dewatering, and pumped discharge lines
- **Cofferdam and isolation work** — isolating a work area from surface water
- **Freeze and frost protection** of active systems through winter work

## Section — Sediment, erosion and discharge

**Heading:** `Erosion and Sediment Control, and Discharge Compliance`

```
Pumping water out of an excavation is the easy part. Where it is allowed to go
is what the permit is about.

- **Erosion and sediment control plans** implemented on the ground, and maintained as the work front moves
- **Silt fence, wattles, check dams and sediment traps** installed progressively as ground is exposed, not after the first rain
- **Settling tanks, bags and ponds** to bring pumped water inside discharge limits before release
- **Filtration and treatment** where settling alone will not achieve the turbidity limit
- **Turbidity monitoring and sampling** to demonstrate compliance rather than assume it
- **Stabilised discharge points** with energy dissipation so the outlet does not become the erosion problem
- **Progressive stabilisation** — seeding, matting, mulch and cover on exposed ground as soon as an area is finished
- **Site inspection and record keeping** so the owner has an environmental record for the project

**On fisheries-sensitive work:** where the receiving environment is a
fish-bearing watercourse, the discharge standard and the consequence of getting
it wrong both change substantially. We work inside the permit conditions and
timing windows, take direction from the environmental monitor where one is
assigned, and would rather stop and fix a control than push water into a creek
and deal with it afterwards.
```

## Section — Why this matters

**Heading:** `Why We Treat This as Its Own Discipline`

```
Most contractors treat dewatering as a pump rental and sediment control as a
roll of silt fence. That works until the day it does not — and the day it does
not, the exposure sits with the owner as much as with the contractor.

We plan water management before mobilisation, price it honestly rather than
hoping the hole stays dry, and maintain the controls as the work front moves.
It is a small cost inside a project and it is the difference between an
excavation that proceeds and one that turns into a regulatory conversation.

If your project has a high water table, a nearby watercourse, contaminated
ground or a tight discharge condition, raise it at tender stage. Those are the
conditions where a low bid that ignored the water becomes the most expensive
option on the table.
```
